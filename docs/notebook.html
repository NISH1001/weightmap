Can a big model's knowledge be mapped into a small one?

A tutorial on what we did, why, how the maths works, and what we found.

Verdict in four lines

1. A simple linear map between a small and a large language model's internal states exists. It looks strong (R² ≈ 0.66), but half to three quarters of that comes from both models simply seeing the same token.

2. The map carries directions: a "looping" direction found in Qwen-1.5B and carried into SmolLM2-135M cuts its loops from 21% to 10%.

3. It does not carry computation: a transplanted block of the big model is worse than deleting the small model's block, and a sparse weight edit's trigger neurons have no counterpart (R² 0.05).

4. It never transferred ability. In one line: models share a coordinate system for what they represent, not for how they compute.

1The question

In November 2026 the "Sparks of Weight-Space Programming" post showed an AI agent (Astra) editing the weights of Qwen3.5-0.8B by hand, with no training, to stop it looping. Nathan Barry then showed that the fix is essentially one direction ("looping minus productive") and that a linear program can find an equivalent edit touching only 8 weight columns.

That raised a bigger question, which became this project:

The hypotheses

  • H1 Models of different sizes live in related regions of a shared space.
  • H2 A map F exists between the small model (S) and the large model (L).
  • H3 S is roughly contained in L.
  • H4 F can carry L's ability into S without training ("reverse distillation").
  • H5 F composes: tiny → small → large chains work.

One rule held throughout: no training. Every experiment uses only forward passes, closed-form least squares, and (at the end) linear programs.

2Primer: weights, activations, and the residual stream

A transformer language model is a stack of blocks. Each token carries a vector, the residual stream h, through the stack. Every block reads h, computes something with its weights (attention and an MLP), and adds the result back:

h₍ℓ+1₎ = h₍ℓ₎ + Block_ℓ(h₍ℓ₎) Block = attention + MLP, built from weight matrices

So there are two different things you could try to map between models:

  • Activations (representations): the vectors h the models produce on the same text. This is what each model represents.
  • Weights: the matrices inside the blocks. This is how each model computes.

Raw weights of different-sized models cannot be compared directly. They have different shapes, and any neuron ordering or rotation gives an equivalent model. So we built the map on activations first, then asked whether it also works on weights (section 8).

3Building the map

Step 1 · Line up the tokens

Different model families split text into different tokens. "unbelievable" may be one token in one model and three in another. We pair positions where both tokenizers end a token at the same character. At those points both models have read exactly the same text, so their states are comparable. Same-family pairs (Qwen 0.5B and 1.5B) share a tokenizer, so every token lines up.

Step 2 · Fit a linear map in closed form

Stack the paired states into matrices: X (one model, n tokens × d₁) and Y (other model, n × d₂). Ridge regression finds the matrix W that best predicts Y from X, with a small penalty that keeps it stable:

W* = argmin_W ‖Y − XW‖² + λ‖W‖² = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹ XᵀY ← one matrix solve, no gradient descent

λ is picked on a held-out slice of the training texts.

Step 3 · Score it honestly

We measure the map with R² on texts it never saw:

R² = 1 − Σ‖yₜ − ŷₜ‖² / Σ‖yₜ − ȳ‖² 1 = perfect, 0 = no better than predicting the average

Holding out whole texts matters. Tokens within one text are very similar, so a token-level split leaks answers. Our first experiment reported R² = 0.85; after switching to text-level holdout and removing chat-template tokens, the real number was about 0.66.

Step 4 · Choose which layers to pair

Linear CKA scores how similar two layers' representations are (1 = same up to rotation and scale):

CKA(X, Y) = ‖XᵀY‖²_F / (‖XᵀX‖_F · ‖YᵀY‖_F) (X, Y centred)

Matches come out in depth order (Qwen-0.5B layer 10 ↔ Qwen-1.5B layer 13, 14 ↔ 17). Picking layers by R² instead gives the same quality.

4Is the map real?

Several quick checks said yes:

  • It is linear. Adding 4,096 random nonlinear features made it slightly worse (0.638 → 0.605).
  • It looks like pruning. Keeping just 896 of L's 1,536 coordinates (picked by importance) predicts S as well as all of them (0.641 vs 0.638). 896 random coordinates give 0.577.
  • It works across families. SmolLM2-360M ← Qwen-1.5B reaches 0.66–0.68.
  • A weight-level version is weak. Mapping the embedding tables directly gives only 0.31–0.42.

Then the control that changed the story. If both models see the word "the", their states are partly predictable just from that fact. How much of R² is only that?

Takeaway. The map is real, but most of its headline number is shared vocabulary. The part that reflects shared understanding of context is modest.

5The atlas and the chains

Atlas: more capable small models map better

We mapped five small models from four families into Qwen-1.5B and compared map quality with each model's capability, measured as bits per byte on the same texts (lower is better; this measure ignores tokenizer differences).

Chains: maps compose

We chained Qwen-1.5B → Qwen-0.5B → SmolLM2-360M → SmolLM2-135M. If each step lost information independently, the composed map should score about the product of the step scores. It doesn't. It keeps about 95% of a direct map.

6Can the map move ability?

The first experiment asked the central question directly. Qwen-1.5B solves 38.7% of MATH problems, Qwen-0.5B 20.7%. Of the problems only L solves, 62% are "blocked" (S solves them in at least one of 8 tries) and the rest "absent" (S never does).

We took L's direction for "solving correctly vs failing", mapped it into S, and pushed S's residual stream along it during generation (steering):

Nothing helped. Zero of 9 "absent" problems were fixed by any method. L's and S's directions point the same way (cosine 0.85), so the map is not carrying anything S lacks. We also confirmed that writing the steering vector into a bias (fold) is exactly equivalent to steering (difference 1.4×10⁻⁴), so a steering result is also a weight-edit result.

7Can the map move a behavior?

If ability doesn't move, maybe a narrower behavior does. SmolLM2-135M falls into repetition loops on GSM8K; Qwen-1.5B doesn't. We labeled S's outputs as looping or productive, built the loop direction twice (once from S's own states, once from L's states mapped into S), and steered S against each.

Takeaway. A behavior direction transfers through the map. It gives no advantage over what S can find in itself, and adds no ability.

8Weight-space tests

Everything so far works on activations. Weight-space programming edits weights. So the remaining question is whether F is a map between the models or only between their representations. Two tests.

8.1 · Block transplant

If F really translates between models, we should be able to run one of L's blocks inside S by translating in and out:

x_S ──A──▶ L coordinates ──Block_L (L's own weights)──▶ ──B──▶ S coordinates

We replace one block of Qwen-0.5B this way and measure its loss on held-out text. A useful scale: 0% means "as bad as deleting the block", 100% means "as good as S's own block".

8.2 · Porting a sparse weight edit (the linear-programming route)

Barry's edit is a rank-1 change to one MLP output matrix (down_proj):

ΔW = δ · u vᵀ u = loop direction ("what to push") v = sparse trigger over MLP neurons ("when to push") v from a linear program on the model's own neuron activations hₜ: minimise Σⱼ |vⱼ| + λ Σₜ slackₜ subject to v·hₜ ≤ −1 on looping tokens (push hard) |v·hₜ| ≤ ε on productive tokens (stay quiet) then keep the 8 strongest columns and re-solve.

The L1 objective makes v sparse, so the LP picks the columns as well as the amounts. To port L's edit into S we carry both halves across. The direction u goes through the activation map. For the trigger, a second LP finds the 8 S neurons that best reproduce L's trigger values on the same text, without using any S loop labels:

port LP: minimise Σ|cⱼ| + λ Σ slack subject to |c·hₜ^S − v_L·hₜ^L| ≤ ε on every aligned token

10Where this sits

The weight-space programming wave on Hacker News (November 2026) is large: hand-edited loop fixes, hidden triggers, output-head edits, AI-written MNIST and Breakout networks, and big LLMs acting as agents that edit small ones. In the nine most relevant posts we read, every edit is built for one model at a time. One post notes "each model needs its own fit" without testing it. None maps between models or tries to port an edit.

So our contribution is the portability question: can a weight-space program found in one model work in another? Our answer is that the direction half ports and the neuron half doesn't. This agrees with a line quoted in another post: editing weights "could remove an obstacle that hid an ability, but it could not add an ability the model lacked."

11Caveats and next steps

  • Scale. All models are 1.5B parameters or smaller. Larger models might share more.
  • Small samples. Loop tests use 200 problems; the atlas has 5 models; the chain result is one seed.
  • One behavior. Only looping was tested as a portable program. Tone or refusal directions may behave differently.
  • The hybrid result is borderline (p ≈ 0.07). It recovers about 55% of S's own edit, consistent with the earlier steering result (10% vs 6.5%).

Possible next steps: rerun the atlas with 10 models and the chains with 5 seeds (scripts written); port a second behavior such as refusal; test a 3B model as L; then write the blog post with the transplant and LP-port figures at its centre.

Glossary

Residual stream
The vector each token carries through the model; every block reads it and adds to it.
Ridge regression
Least-squares fit with a small penalty on weight size; solved with one matrix inversion.
R²
Share of variance a prediction explains on held-out data. 1 is perfect, 0 is "predict the mean".
CKA
Similarity score between two sets of representations, insensitive to rotation and scale.
Steering
Adding a fixed vector to the residual stream during generation.
Fold
Writing a steering vector into a layer's bias, turning it into a weight edit with identical effect.
Linear program (LP)
Optimisation with a linear objective and linear constraints; with an L1 objective it finds sparse solutions.
Trigger / payload
In a rank-1 edit u vᵀ, v decides when the edit fires, u decides what it adds.
AUC
Probability that a randomly chosen positive (looping) token scores beyond a random negative one; 0.5 is chance.
Bits per byte
Next-token loss normalised by text length in bytes; lets models with different tokenizers be compared.
